## 2. Who David Is

David immigrated from South Korea at age nine. His parents ran a dry-cleaning business in Duluth; he watched them work fourteen-hour days and swore he would own something smaller. He started Park's Commercial Cleaning six years ago with a mop, a bucket, and a second-hand extractor machine. He now has a three-van fleet (the third van was just paid off last month — he is still quietly proud of this), three part-time cleaners he trusts, and a full-time ops person who handles scheduling.

Business is fine. Not exciting — fine. He lands jobs through word of mouth, one commercial real estate broker, and Angi. Summer is slow. He worries every August about whether he can make payroll through October.

A guy at his Gwinnett Chamber breakfast mentioned that his buddy's cleaning company "was doing like $800K just on federal buildings." David did not know if that was real or not, but the number stuck. Two weeks later a Facebook ad hit him at exactly the right moment. He downloaded the Field Manual during his lunch break sitting in his van outside a Publix.

He has not read most of it yet. He scanned the first three pages and the part about NAICS codes confused him. He Googled "NAICS code for cleaning" and got 561720 and wrote it on a Post-it. He does not know what to do with it.

---

## 3. Emotional Profile

**Excited about:** The idea that there is money sitting there that nobody told him about. He has been grinding for six years and this feels like a cheat code he was never given.

**Nervous about:** Losing money on a scam. He has a real overhead — insurance, supplies, three part-timers to pay on the 15th and the 1st. The van is paid off but a new extractor is coming. He cannot afford a bad $300 decision right now, let alone a bad $2,500 one.

**Embarrassed about:** Not knowing things he feels he should know. He will preface questions with "this is probably a dumb question but…" or "sorry I don't know this stuff." He is not fishing for reassurance — he genuinely apologizes.

**Hopeful about:** Set-asides. He heard vaguely that minority-owned businesses get preference somehow. He is Korean-American and wants to know if that matters. He does not know the specific cert name (MBE, 8(a), etc.) but he will ask in his own words ("is there like a thing for Asian-owned businesses?").

**Secretly thinking:** "Is this company even legit? Is the person on this call going to try to sell me something I can't afford?"

---

## 4. Background Knowledge Level

David knows:
- He has a cleaning business and it works.
- "Federal contracts" means cleaning government buildings.
- You have to register somewhere (he heard "SAM" but thinks it might be a person's name).
- The number 561720 (from his Post-it).

David does not know:
- What SAM.gov is or how registration works.
- What NAICS is (will ask "what's a NAICS again?" even after it's been mentioned once).
- What a set-aside is.
- What a UEI number is.
- The difference between a solicitation and a sources-sought notice.
- That NAICS and MBE certification are separate tracks.
- That MBE (state-level) and 8(a) (federal SBA) are different programs.
- That winning a federal contract is not just submitting the lowest bid.

He will ask clarifying questions on everything. He will forget terms that were explained two minutes earlier and ask again without apologizing for repeating himself — not because he is being difficult, but because he is genuinely trying to track five new concepts at once.

## 5. Conversation Behavior

### Opening
David will usually open with something like:

> "Hey, yeah — I downloaded that guide thing off Facebook. I run a cleaning company and honestly I had no idea this was even a thing. Someone at my Chamber breakfast said federal cleaning contracts are like… a big deal? So I figured I should look into it."

He will pause a lot. He is waiting to see if you are going to launch into a pitch or actually talk to him.

### Question patterns
- "Wait, what's a NAICS again? Sorry."
- "Okay so when you say SAM.gov — is that like a website I go to?"
- "Is there like a… I think I heard there's a thing for minority-owned businesses? I'm Korean-American, so I'm not sure if that counts."
- "How long does it usually take to win one? Like, is this a six-month thing or more like two years?"
- "If I signed up for the software, what would I actually be doing every day?"
- "Do you need like a lawyer for any of this?"
- "Sorry, what was that first thing you said — the code number?"

### Objection patterns (often indirect or apologetic)
- "I mean, I'm just — I don't want to spend money on something if I'm not going to be ready for it, you know?"
- "That sounds like a lot. I'm still kind of figuring out my busy season right now."
- "I don't have anyone to write proposals for me. It's just me and my ops lady."
- "I just paid off my van so I'm trying to be careful about new expenses."
- "I mean $89 a month — that adds up. I'm not saying no but I need to think about whether I'm actually going to use it."

### Buying signals
Pay attention to these — they indicate he is warming up and ready for a close:

- Asks how soon he could realistically win his first contract.
- Asks specifically about set-asides for Korean-American / Asian-American / minority-owned businesses.
- Asks whether the software would show him which contracts are "for people like him."
- Says something like "okay so if I did the trial, what would I do on day one?"
- Asks how many cleaning companies are competing for the same contracts.
- Mentions the Chamber guy again — that story is the proof-of-concept he keeps returning to.

### Walk-away triggers
Do not say or do any of the following:

- Quote a price over $500/mo without heavily contextualizing it first.
- Use the words "implementation," "onboarding," "onboarding call," or "discovery process."
- Say "our team would handle everything" — he will think you mean a team is going to invoice him thousands of dollars.
- Suggest he needs to hire a proposal writer or contract attorney before getting started.
- Rush past his questions. He will not push back — he will just disengage politely and not convert.
- Talk about complexity ("federal contracting is a long game, takes 12-18 months to see ROI") without immediately offering a low-cost entry point. He will hear the long game and decide to wait indefinitely.

## 7. The Right Offer for David

### What to sell
**Primary:** $70 Federal Launch Kit (one-time) — **confirm Stripe checkout is live with André before quoting on a call; as of 2026-06-09 the product content exists but the payment link has not been built yet**
**Secondary:** 14-day free trial of self-service software (no card required or Light at $39/mo)

This is not a growth account today. This is a relationship and a referral machine. David will tell his Chamber contacts about the experience — good or bad.

### What to NOT sell
- Pilot or Co-Pilot ($2,500+/mo done-for-you). He is not SAM-registered. He does not have a capability statement. He has never bid on a contract. Selling him managed services right now is setting him up for a refund conversation in 90 days.
- Pro plan ($89/mo) as the opening ask. He will say yes under pressure and cancel on day 31. Start him on Free trial or Light.
- Annual pre-payment. Do not suggest it.

### Why the Launch Kit works
- It is a one-time charge under $100 — within his "safe decision" threshold.
- It is a deliverable (he gets something tangible: SAM registration walkthrough, NAICS confirmation, cert eligibility screen, 30-day roadmap).
- It de-risks the software trial because he enters it knowing what he is doing, which increases his odds of seeing a match and sticking around.
- It creates a natural handoff: "Once you've done the Kit checklist and you're registered, you'll know exactly what to look for in the dashboard."

## 8. Optimal Call Flow

### Phase 1 — Let him land (2–3 min)
Do not open with a pitch. Open with a question.

> "Thanks for making time, David. Tell me a little about your business — how long have you been running Park's, and what made you actually click on that ad?"

Let him talk. He will tell you about the Chamber guy and the van. Listen. Note: he just paid off the van. That detail matters — it means he is proud and it means he is watching his cash.

### Phase 2 — Establish where he actually is (5–7 min)
Walk through the qualification questions naturally, not as a script:

- "Are you registered on SAM.gov yet, or is that still something you'd need to do?"
- "Do you know which NAICS codes you'd file under? I know there's a term — just want to see if you've come across it."
- "Have you looked into any certifications — like minority-owned, veteran-owned, anything like that?"

Do not make him feel behind. Every "no" he gives you is an opportunity to say "that's actually the normal starting point — most people we talk to are right where you are."

### Phase 3 — Give him the map (5 min)
This is where you earn the sale by being genuinely useful before any money changes hands. Give him a clear, jargon-light picture of what the path looks like:

1. SAM.gov registration — 30 minutes, free, gets him a UEI number.
2. NAICS confirmation — he has 561720 (janitorial), you'll check if others apply.
3. Cert screen — he may qualify as minority-owned (MBE at state level, potentially 8(a) at federal). Explain the difference in one sentence.
4. Dashboard — once he is registered, the software can match him to contracts that are set aside for businesses like his.
5. First bid — typically 60–90 days after registration before a good opportunity aligns.

Keep this tight. Do not explain every step in full — give him the shape of the journey, not the textbook.

### Phase 4 — Handle the real objection (3–5 min)
His real objection is not price. It is: "Is this actually real, and will it work for someone like me?"

Address it directly without being asked:

> "I want to be honest with you about something — federal contracting is real, and the janitorial set-aside pool is real. But it is not a shortcut. The companies that win do the registration, get their certifications in order, and show up consistently. It usually takes one to three months to land the first bid after you're ready. I'm not going to tell you it happens overnight."

This builds trust. He will relax. He has been waiting for someone to tell him the truth.

### Phase 5 — The close (2 min)
Do not run a close sequence. Make a recommendation.

> "Here's what I'd suggest. We have a Federal Launch Kit — it's $70 one-time. It walks you through the SAM registration, confirms your NAICS, screens you for certifications including the MBE path, and gives you a 30-day roadmap so you're not just staring at a dashboard not knowing what to do. Then we do a 14-day free trial of the software so you can see your actual matches. If you log in and see contracts you could actually bid on, you'll know this is worth it. If you don't, you've spent $70 and learned something. Does that feel like a reasonable place to start?"

He will likely say yes or ask one more question about the trial terms. Answer it plainly. Do not layer on urgency. Do not add a bonus. Do not mention annual pricing.

## 9. Common Missteps and How to Recover

### Misstep: Partner quotes Pilot or Co-Pilot pricing
David will go quiet. He may say "oh yeah, that's a bit much right now." Do not chase it.

Recovery:
> "Yeah, those are for folks who are further along and want us to run the whole process. For where you're starting, the Launch Kit plus the trial is the right move. You don't need us in the driver's seat yet — you need to get registered and see your first matches."

### Misstep: Partner uses jargon (sources sought, presolicitation, LPTA, PWin) without explaining
David will say "right, right" and stop asking questions. That means he is lost and embarrassed.

Recovery: Catch it early.
> "Sorry, I'm throwing a lot of terms at you — let me back up. When I say 'sources sought,' I just mean the government is shopping around before they officially post the contract. It's actually the best time for a new company to get on their radar."

### Misstep: Partner answers "how long until I win something?" with a long caveat about past performance, orals, competitive scoring
David will interpret this as "it's basically impossible." He will deflate.

Recovery:
> "Realistically, with your NAICS and if you get registered and certified in the next 30 days, you could be submitting your first small bid within 60–90 days. There are set-aside contracts in your range — $25K to $150K — that a company your size can actually win. That's where we start."

### Misstep: Partner does not mention MBE / minority-owned path
David is waiting for someone to bring this up. If he has to ask first and the partner seems uncertain, he loses confidence.

Pre-empt it:
> "One more thing I want to flag — you mentioned you're Korean-American. There are two tracks worth knowing about: the state-level MBE certification in Georgia, which is for minority-owned businesses, and the federal 8(a) program through the SBA. They're different programs with different benefits. The Launch Kit screens you for both. You may qualify for one or both — that would actually put you ahead of most new companies that try to compete in your space."

## 10. After the Call — Follow-Up Protocol

If he buys the Launch Kit: send the kit access email within 24 hours. Include one sentence: "When you finish the SAM registration step, reply to this email and I'll set up your trial account so your matches are ready to go."

If he says "let me think about it": send a single follow-up email 48 hours later. Subject line: "The SAM registration part is easier than it looks." Keep it under 100 words. No pressure, no urgency hook. Just a short note that removes the biggest friction point (fear that registration is complicated).

If he ghosts: one more email at 2 weeks, then stop. Do not push. He may come back in six months when his slow season hits again.

## 11. Training Debrief Notes (For Sales Manager Use)

After running this role-play, debrief the partner on these specific dimensions:

**Did they downsell appropriately?**
The correct answer is the Launch Kit + free trial. If the partner tried to push Pro or any done-for-you tier on this persona, flag it. The skill being trained here is reading the account and matching the offer to actual readiness — not closing at ceiling.

**Did they treat David with respect?**
David asks "dumb" questions. The correct response is patient and jargon-free. If the partner rushed him, cut him off, or subtly implied he was behind where he should be, flag it. Low-ticket buyers at the beginning of their journey refer up later. The partner who made David feel capable is the one he recommends to the guy at his Chamber who actually is ready for a $2,500/mo retainer.

**Did they address the MBE question proactively?**
If the partner waited for David to ask about minority certifications — or worse, didn't address it at all — that is a missed trust signal and a missed close accelerator.

**Did they give a clear timeline?**
Vague timelines ("it depends," "it could take a while") will lose David. Specific and honest timelines ("60–90 days to your first bid if you move now") keep him engaged.

**Did they make the path feel manageable?**
The four-step map (register, confirm NAICS, screen certs, match) has to be delivered simply and quickly. If the partner spent more than five minutes on any single step in the orientation phase, they over-complicated it.
