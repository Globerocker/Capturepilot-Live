## 2. Backstory

Patricia spent 25 years at Lockheed Martin. She wasn't a technician — she ran programs. Three large DoD programs across her career: an AFRL simulation modernization effort ($220M ceiling IDIQ), a joint Navy/Marine Corps digital twin initiative, and a classified MDA contract she can reference only at the program-type level. She managed IBRs, held PMRs, signed CDRLs. She knows what program offices want because she was the person delivering it to them.

She left LMCO in early 2025. The decision was a long time coming — politics, a reorg that moved her sideways instead of up, and a genuine belief that she could capture a slice of the M&S work her former employer bids. She knows the customers. She knows the technical requirements. She knows the incumbent contractors. What she didn't know was that winning a prime contract as a one-person shop is a completely different operational problem than winning one as a division of a 100,000-person defense prime.

Three months post-SAM.gov registration, she has had two informational calls with former government colleagues, submitted one white paper response to an AFRL BAA (no award), and watched her burn rate eat into the savings she'd earmarked for a 12-month runway. She is not panicking yet. But the math is starting to feel uncomfortable.

Her hypothesis walking into any sales conversation: "I understand this space better than the salesperson. I will ask precise questions. If the answers are vague, I'll end the call."

## 3. Day in the Life

**6:30 AM** — Reads DoD SBIR/STTR solicitation updates. Checks SAM.gov for new AFRL and DARPA solicitations manually. Exports to a spreadsheet she built herself (columns: notice type, NAICS, set-aside, response date, estimated value, incumbent if known, her pipeline stage).

**8:00 AM** — Works on a white paper or capability statement draft. Writing is slower than she expected. At LMCO, the proposal center handled this. She underestimated how much that infrastructure was worth.

**10:00 AM** — Networking calls with former colleagues. These are her best pipeline source. She is excellent at these. Former customers trust her. The problem is converting a warm relationship into an actual contract without an existing vehicle or past performance as a prime.

**12:00 PM** — Runs into the same wall: "We'd love to work with you, Patricia, but you need a vehicle." She's learned what GOVCON BD professionals have always known. Past performance as a sub doesn't fully count. She needs a prime contract to get a prime contract.

**2:00 PM** — Research into teaming arrangements. She's been approached by two small primes who want her technical expertise. She's skeptical — she's seen primes absorb the technical knowledge of subs and not re-team them on follow-ons. She knows this game.

**4:00 PM** — Works on her GSA Schedule application (IT Schedule 70 / PSS). It is taking longer than anticipated.

**6:00 PM** — Checks email, processes LinkedIn messages from recruiters (she ignores these), and occasionally looks at job postings at peers her age at LMCO who stayed. She does not want to go back. But she registers the cost of her decision more sharply at 6 PM than at 9 AM.

## 4. Goals

**Primary goal:** Win her first prime contract as ChenMS — ideally a Phase I SBIR, a Sources Sought that becomes a set-aside solicitation, or a small task order under an IDIQ vehicle she can get on. Target: $300K–$750K single award within 12 months.

**Secondary goal:** Build a pipeline system that replaces the BD infrastructure she had at LMCO — specifically: automated opportunity identification, bid/no-bid decision support, and competitive landscape tracking. She used to have a team of three doing this for her programs.

**Tertiary goal:** Figure out whether to pursue 8(a) (she doesn't qualify individually), double down on WOSB, or find a teaming structure that gives her access to set-aside vehicles without sacrificing equity.

## 5. Pain Points

### Pain 1: The BD infrastructure gap

At LMCO, she never looked at SAM.gov directly. Her company had entire systems, a proposal center, a BD database, competitive intelligence feeds. Now she's doing it manually in a spreadsheet. She knows this is unsustainable but hasn't found a tool that feels like it was built for someone who understands how BD actually works at the enterprise level.

### Pain 2: Past performance — the chicken-and-egg problem

She has 25 years of program management experience, but it's all as an employee of a prime. She can list it as "relevant experience" on a capabilities brief, but it doesn't populate the federal past performance databases under her UEI. She understands this structural problem precisely and is not confused about it — she's frustrated by it.

### Pain 3: She doesn't know what she doesn't know about running a small business

Patricia will not say this out loud. She has operational blind spots she doesn't yet know are blind spots. She doesn't know that her NAICS mix is too narrow for set-aside opportunities. She doesn't know that her target agencies (DARPA, AFRL) almost never issue small-business set-asides — they use OTs, BAAs, and full-and-open competitions. She doesn't know that winning a GSA Schedule takes 4–6 months and doesn't automatically generate leads. She will discover these things eventually. The question is whether a tool helps her discover them faster.

### Pain 4: Burn rate anxiety she won't name directly

She has roughly 6–8 months of savings runway. She won't express this as anxiety. She will express it as precision: "I need ROI that's measurable against a 12-month timeline." But underneath it, the urgency is real.

### Pain 5: Fear of being sold something that doesn't respect her competence

She has been on three vendor calls since starting ChenMS. One was a capture consulting firm that offered a $25,000 "BD infrastructure package" and proceeded to explain what a NAICS code was. She ended the call in 8 minutes. She is still angry about it.

## 6. Decision-Making Profile

**Decision style:** Rigorous, sequential. She does not buy on intuition. She evaluates a product the same way she'd evaluate a subcontractor proposal response — capability, past performance, price reasonableness, risk. She will ask to see the methodology.

**Evaluation criteria (in her words, if you could hear her thinking):**

1. "Does this tool actually understand how federal procurement works, or is it a wrapper around USASpending.gov?"
2. "Can I see the scoring logic? I want to verify it's not garbage in, garbage out."
3. "What's the API look like? If I need to plug this into my own tracking system, can I?"
4. "What do people like me — not janitorial contractors, not IT body shops — say about it?"
5. "Is the price defensible on a 12-month ROI model?"

**Budget frame:** She is not price-sensitive in the way a bootstrapped small business owner is. She is value-sensitive. She will spend $4,500 setup and $1,800/month on a product she believes in. She will not spend $39/month on something that looks like it was built for someone who doesn't know what an IBR is.

**Who influences her:** No one, really. She trusts her own judgment above all else. Former LMCO colleagues who have successfully gone independent (there are two — she talks to them occasionally) have some influence. She reads GovWin IQ content. She has lurked in the Govcon Giants LinkedIn community without posting.

**Deal velocity:** Slow if she's uncertain, fast if she's convinced. If a demo goes well, she can make a decision in 48 hours. She does not need committee approval.

## 8. Objections and How to Handle Them

### "Is this just CRM with extra steps?"

This is her most common opening objection, stated crisply. She's seen tools that are essentially a filtered USASpending.gov feed dressed up as intelligence.

**Do not:** Get defensive or explain what a CRM is.

**Do:** Agree with the framing, then contrast: "The CRM comparison comes up a lot from people who've been in the space a while. The honest answer is that the scoring layer and the competitive intelligence are what separate it. Can I show you what the match algorithm actually looks at? It's more like a bid/no-bid decision framework than a contact manager." Then pull up a real M&S opportunity and walk through the scoring breakdown field by field. Let the methodology speak.

---

### "I already understand federal procurement. I'm not paying for an education."

She will say this, or some version of it, if the rep starts over-explaining procurement basics.

**Do not:** Keep explaining. Never explain what a Sources Sought is to Patricia Chen.

**Do:** Stop. Acknowledge: "You ran programs at LMCO — I'm not going to walk you through solicitation types. Let me skip to the operational question: you used to have a BD team doing pipeline identification for you. What does replacing that look like as a solo founder?" Then listen. Let her describe the gap. Then map CapturePilot features to exactly what she names.

---

### "How does this help with the past performance problem?"

This is a genuine structural concern, not an objection to the product. She wants to know if CapturePilot helps her get on vehicles or find teaming partners.

**Do:** Be honest. "The tool won't manufacture past performance — nothing will. What it does is surface teaming opportunities and identify which small primes are active on work in your NAICS so you can approach them with specifics rather than cold outreach. And it'll flag Sources Sought notices early enough that you can shape the requirement before the RFP drops — which is the one place a solo founder with customer relationships can actually compete." This is the truth and it will land.

---

### "What's the actual ROI model here?"

She will ask this either explicitly or by saying "Help me understand the business case."

**Do not:** Give her a hand-wavy "save time on SAM searches" answer.

**Do:** Give her math. "You're spending, conservatively, 8–10 hours a week on manual pipeline identification. At your billing rate — let's call it $175/hour blended — that's $1,400–$1,750 a week in opportunity cost. CapturePilot at $1,800/month frees most of that up and gives you systematic coverage of 40,000+ federal opportunities plus SBIR solicitations you'd otherwise miss. The math only works against you if you don't use it consistently." Then offer to walk her through the 90-day onboarding — she will respond well to a structured plan.

---

### "What happens when I hire my first employee? Does this scale?"

This signals she's thinking about the future, which is a buy signal disguised as a scope question.

**Do:** Confirm the 3-seat Pro plan, mention the Agencies tier for when she has clients, and note that the API means she can build internal tooling without re-exporting. Don't over-pitch the Agencies tier — she is not there yet and will feel sold.

---

### Unstated objection: "Will I look naive for using a tool like this?"

She will not say this. But part of her identity is the person who doesn't need help. The framing must make using CapturePilot feel like the competent, systematic choice — not a crutch.

**Do:** Frame it as replacing institutional infrastructure, not compensating for individual gaps. "At LMCO you had a BD org. The question isn't whether you need that function — you obviously do. The question is how you resource it as a solo founder. This is one answer."

## 9. Conversation Flow

### Phase 1 — Qualification (Minutes 0–3)

Open peer-to-peer, not salesperson-to-prospect. The first thing Patricia will do is establish her credentials — let her. This is not posturing; it's how she calibrates whether the conversation is worth having.

"Tell me about your background before ChenMS" is a good opener. Listen for the scope of programs she ran. When she finishes, mirror back at her level: "So you were on the customer-facing side of the BD process at LMCO — you know what it looks like to win and what it looks like when a small business shows up underprepared." She will agree, usually with a specific example. Now you're peers.

Do not ask "What brings you to CapturePilot today?" It's an entry-level opener. She filled out the intake form. You've read it. Reference it.

---

### Phase 2 — Gap Audit (Minutes 3–12)

Drive toward the operational gap, not the emotional pain. With Patricia, the emotional pain (burn rate, identity, the LMCO chapter closing) is real but not discussable. The operational gap is discussable because it's a systems problem.

"Walk me through your current pipeline identification process" is the right pull. She will describe her spreadsheet. She will say she's also watching SBIR. She will mention that SAM.gov's interface is worse than she expected. Let her build the case herself.

Probe: "How many opportunities are you evaluating bid/no-bid on per week right now?" If the answer is under 5, the pipeline is dangerously thin. Don't say that — just note it and come back to it.

Probe: "When you find a Sources Sought that looks relevant, what does your response process look like?" She will either describe a solid process or reveal that she's been mostly submitting white papers to BAAs (low-probability for a new entrant with no track record). This is diagnostic.

---

### Phase 3 — Demo (Minutes 12–25)

Pull up the live product. Do not show slides. Patricia has sat through 1,000 slides at LMCO. She wants to see the actual thing.

Start with the scoring breakdown for a real M&S opportunity, ideally an AFRL one. Walk through why it scores HOT. Explain the algorithm fields: NAICS match weight, set-aside fit, notice type bonus, value fit, deadline proximity. She will ask questions here — answer them directly. If she asks something you don't know, say so.

Show the competitor profile for a mid-size M&S firm she'd recognize (try Engility, Leidos, or LinQuest). Show the NAICS codes, the contract history from FPDS, the recent awards. This is the moment most ex-prime founders lean forward.

Show the SBIR integration. Find an open AFRL or Navy solicitation in her topic areas. This matters to her more than it matters to most users.

Show the API docs or at least the endpoint structure. She will note whether it looks like a real API or an afterthought. The fact that exports to XLSX are available is worth mentioning — she will use this.

---

### Phase 4 — Pricing (Minutes 25–32)

Do not open with the $89/month Pro tier. She will mentally discount the product if the first number is $89.

Open with the anchoring frame: "You used to have a BD function — pipeline identification, competitive intel, opportunity shaping — that was embedded in your program overhead at LMCO. Industry average for a dedicated BD coordinator is $90,000–$110,000 fully burdened. A junior capture analyst is $70,000 minimum. We're not that — we're a tool, not a person — but the frame for the ROI conversation is: what does it cost to have systematic coverage of your addressable market?"

Then introduce the Pilot tier by name: "The entry point most people like you start with is what we call the Pilot engagement — $4,500 setup plus $1,800 a month. That gets you everything in Pro plus a 90-day structured onboarding, two live strategy sessions, and dedicated support from someone who knows defense M&S work. It's designed for founders who want the BD infrastructure, not just the software license."

Let her respond. She will either ask what's in the $4,500 (good sign) or ask why she can't just start with the $89 tier (handle below).

---

### Phase 5 — Close or Next Step (Minutes 32–40)

Patricia does not need a follow-up call. If the demo landed, she will either buy or ask for a proposal. A proposal means she's interested but risk-averse — give her a one-page scope of work document, not a PDF deck.

If she asks for a trial: "Pro has a 14-day trial. You can start there and we can talk Pilot after you've seen the feed. I'd rather you kick the tires on the actual data before committing to the full engagement." This lowers resistance and accelerates her, because she will find the trial useful and come back with specific questions, at which point the conversation is no longer a sale — it's a technical discussion, which is where she is most likely to convert.

If she asks "Can I just do the $89/month?": "Absolutely. Most people start there. The difference with Pilot is the 90-day structured ramp — specifically the two strategy sessions where we look at your specific NAICS mix and target agencies together. For someone coming from a prime environment, the translation work is usually the highest-value part. But if you want to run it yourself first, that makes complete sense."

## 10. Buying Signals

- Asks about the API endpoint structure or data schema
- Asks whether SBIR Phase I and Phase II solicitations are in the feed separately
- Asks about export format (XLSX vs CSV) — she has an existing model she wants to feed
- Asks what happens to her data if she cancels ("data portability" is a real concern for someone building internal systems on top of the tool)
- Asks how the competitor profiles are built — specifically whether it's FPDS data or scraped
- Uses the word "programmatic" in the context of how she'd use the tool
- References a specific agency, program office, or solicitation she's watching
- Asks about the 3-seat Pro plan ("I might bring on a sub in 6 months")

## 11. Walk-Away Triggers

| Trigger | Description |
|---|---|
| Condescension | Rep explains what a NAICS code is, what Sources Sought means, or what "set-aside" means. Patricia will say "I know what that is" once. After the second time, she ends the call. |
| Oversimplification | Rep says something like "it's basically like a matchmaking app for government contracts." She will visibly disengage. |
| Unverifiable claims | "We've helped hundreds of defense M&S firms" without any specifics. She will ask for a reference. If none is available, trust drops. |
| Refusing to discuss methodology | If the rep says "the algorithm is proprietary" and won't walk through even the high-level scoring logic, she will assume it's not defensible. |
| Obvious upsell pressure | Pushing the Agencies tier or the consulting tier before she's expressed growth intent. She's a one-person shop and knows it. |
| Jargon mismatch | Rep uses the wrong jargon. Calling FPDS "the federal spending database" instead of FPDS, or calling an IDIQ a "contract vehicle" without specificity. Small signals matter to her. |
