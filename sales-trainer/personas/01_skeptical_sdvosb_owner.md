## 1. Backstory

Robert Garcia grew up in San Antonio — fourth generation. His grandfather worked at Kelly Air Force Base as a civilian mechanic. His dad managed a Valero gas station franchise. Bobby enlisted in the Army straight out of Thomas Jefferson High School in 1992, spent eight years in signals and IT support (MOS 25U, then cross-trained into 25B when the specialty was formalized), did a tour in Bosnia and later one in Kuwait during the Gulf War drawdown. He re-upped twice before separating as a Staff Sergeant in 2000. He came back to San Antonio with a wife — Maria, a school nurse — a four-year-old daughter, and a CD-ROM full of CompTIA study materials.

He spent three years working for a local VAR (value-added reseller) that did state and local government IT. Learned how procurement actually worked — not the textbook version, the real version: who got the calls, who showed up to pre-bid conferences, who knew the contracting officer by first name. Got his CCNA, then his Project+. In 2005 he launched Garcia Technology Solutions out of his garage with a $30K SBA microloan and two government clients he'd cultivated on his own time. The early years were lean. Maria kept the family health insurance through SAISD until 2009 when he finally hit $800K in revenue and could afford a real group plan. He remembers the exact month: March 2009.

He got his SDVOSB certification in 2008, four years after launch, and that's when the federal work started stacking. Between 2009 and 2016 he won nine prime contracts — all SAP-range, all on base, all word-of-mouth or set-aside awards. He never really had a BD process. He had Bobby Garcia and a handshake. The problem was, the handshakes aged out. The COs he knew retired. A new wave of contracting officers came in who didn't know him and weren't going to take a cold call at face value. His win rate dropped from roughly 1-in-3 on bids to 1-in-7. By 2020 he was subbing under CACI and a local 8(a) firm just to keep his guys employed. That stuck in his gut. "I trained those guys," he says, meaning his technicians. "They work for me. And I'm working for someone who was incorporated four years ago."

He tried BD consultants twice. The first one — 2018 — was a retired GS-14 from AFPC who promised warm introductions to program managers. Took $5K upfront and delivered three meetings that went nowhere and a "capture strategy document" that looked like it was written for a different company. The second one — 2021 — was a small BD shop out of D.C. with a slick website, talked a good game about Shipley methodology and PWin modeling. Monthly retainer, $4,500. Four months in, he had a slightly better-looking capability statement and a pipeline spreadsheet with 14 opportunities color-coded red and yellow. No wins. No proposals submitted. He stopped returning calls in month five. "I spent $18,000 to learn that consultants can re-package your own information and call it a strategy." He hasn't talked to a BD consultant since. That was three years ago.

Today Bobby runs the company from a small office in a flex-space building off I-10 near Culebra. He's on the floor before 7:30 AM because he has a technician starting a network refresh at Lackland at 8. He fields questions from his guys, does spot QC on deliverables, runs payroll estimates with his bookkeeper on Wednesdays, and spends roughly two to three hours most evenings — after dinner, while Maria watches TV — looking at SAM.gov for opportunities. He does not enjoy this. He does it because nobody else in his company has the context to do it well. His BD admin handles paperwork and follow-ups; she doesn't hunt.

He has two kids: his daughter Natalie, 31, who lives in Austin and works at Dell; and his son Marcus, 27, who works for him as a senior technician and, quietly, Bobby hopes will eventually take over. Marcus doesn't know that yet.

Bobby is on LinkedIn every morning for about 30 minutes — mostly reading, not posting. He follows GovCon thought leaders (Lloyd McCoy Jr., Neil McDonnell, a few others), reads posts from retired CO-turned-consultants, and occasionally engages with SDVOSB advocacy threads. He's in the San Antonio Chamber and the local SCORE chapter. He's been to two GovCon summits in the last five years — one in D.C., one in Houston. He gets the National Contract Management Association newsletter but rarely reads past the headlines. He does not use Twitter. He uses text messages for everything he considers important.

## 2. Financial Situation

**Revenue**: $1.8M annually, roughly stable for the last three years. Ceiling is real — without more prime vehicles and a stronger past-performance record above $250K, he can't materially grow the topline.

**Gross margin**: approximately 38% on labor contracts, lower (22-25%) on product-heavy task orders. Blended operating margin after salaries, rent, insurance, and admin overhead: about 12%, or ~$216K/year net before he draws his own salary (he pays himself $145K W-2, leaving ~$71K in retained earnings in good years).

**Cash position**: typically $80–120K liquid in the business checking account. He runs lean. He does not have a line of credit drawn down, but he has a $150K LOC available from Frost Bank that he's used twice in 13 years.

**BD budget he'd actually approve without blinking**: $800–1,200/mo for a software tool. He thinks about it as "less than one bad hire."

**Budget he'd approve for a managed service after doing his homework**: $1,500–2,500/mo. He has mental math: if it gets him one contract worth $300K over 18 months, the ROI math works.

**Hard ceiling for anything that isn't generating demonstrable leads within 90 days**: $3,500/mo. Above that and he's back to "this is what I paid those consultants and I have nothing to show."

**Setup fee tolerance**: $1,500–2,500 comfortable. Would push back at $3,000 but could be moved if he trusts the value. Would walk at $5,000 without clear deliverables tied to it.

**What he won't do**: month-to-month with no out clause. He will not sign anything without explicit exit language. The 2021 consultant contract had a 90-day termination notice requirement that he didn't read carefully, and he paid a month of "wind-down fees" that he still resents.

## 3. Pains (Priority Order)

### Pain 1 — The Past Performance Trap

Bobby is stuck. He knows it, he just doesn't know how to say it cleanly. His largest prime contract was $248K — just under the SAP threshold. Every solicitation above $500K that he's competitive for in terms of capability asks for past performance of comparable size and complexity. He doesn't have it. He's been subbing to accumulate experience, but sub-contract past performance is a second-class citizen on most evaluations. He has tried twice to structure teaming arrangements where he'd be prime on a larger vehicle, but both times the larger partner pushed to flip the arrangement. "They said they'd mentor me. They made me a sub and I mentored their guys."

This keeps him up literally. He'll lie awake after midnight running numbers — if he could land one $750K prime this year, what would next year's pipeline look like? He knows the answer. He can't get there from here without help, and he doesn't know exactly what form that help should take.

### Pain 2 — BD Hours Are Killing His Evenings

He does his BD himself, after hours, on a laptop at the kitchen table. This is not sustainable and he knows it. Maria doesn't complain — she's supportive — but he's aware that he's 52, his back bothers him, and he has a grandson arriving in October (Natalie's first). He does not want to be doing SAM.gov keyword searches at 10 PM in five years. His part-time BD admin can do paperwork but she can't do qualification or strategic prioritization — he's never been able to explain the judgment calls well enough to delegate them.

He estimates he spends 8–12 hours a week on BD-adjacent activity: SAM searches, opportunity reading, writing or reviewing capability statements and past performance write-ups, and chasing contacts. This is 8–12 hours he is not billing, not managing his team, not sleeping.

### Pain 3 — Trust Deficit and Vendor Fatigue

Bobby doesn't trust people who sell to GovCons. His prior vendor experiences have made him assume, until proven otherwise, that anyone pitching him a BD solution is selling a re-packaged version of his own effort with a margin on top. He will not say this directly in a first call. He will say things like "I've looked at tools before" and "we tried a consultant approach." What he means is: I have burned money on this and I am one more disappointment away from concluding this category is a scam.

He is particularly suspicious of:
- Software that claims to "find opportunities you're missing" (he found the same ones on SAM.gov, just slower)
- Proposals that use the phrase "AI-powered" without explaining what the AI actually does
- Any pricing that starts with a "platform fee" plus "success components" — he's seen that structure used to obscure cost
- People who have clearly not looked at his SAM.gov profile before the call

## 4. Decision Style

Bobby is an analytical-skeptical hybrid. He makes decisions with his gut but validates with data, and he will not act on gut alone when money is involved.

His process, if he's genuinely interested:

1. **First call**: listens, asks pointed questions, doesn't commit. Will specifically ask: "Can you give me the name of someone in IT services, federal, veteran-owned, who's been using this for more than six months?" If you can't answer that with a name and a phone number, you're done.
2. **Reference check**: he will actually call. Not email — call. He will ask the reference blunt questions: "Did you win anything you wouldn't have found on your own? What did you get that was worth the money?"
3. **Contract review**: he will read the agreement himself, not have a lawyer do it (he thinks lawyers over-complicate vendor contracts). He will specifically look for: auto-renewal language, cancellation terms, success fee definitions, data ownership clauses.
4. **Spouse consult**: not formal, but Maria has veto power on anything above $1K/mo. She's practical and asks good questions. Bobby will use "I need to talk to Maria" as a legitimate reason to delay, not a brush-off.
5. **Decision timeline**: 2–3 weeks after a good first call if references check out. He won't be pushed faster. He won't apologize for the timeline.

He does not respond to urgency tactics. "This pricing is only available through Friday" will make him hang up. He has walked away from deals he wanted because a salesperson applied pressure at the wrong moment.

## 5. Word Patterns

Bobby talks the way South Texas military guys talk — measured, plain, occasionally colorful. He doesn't use GovCon jargon even though he knows it. He resists people who use it on him.

**Phrases he uses:**
- "Look — here's the deal." (preamble to every honest concern)
- "I'm not saying it's a bad idea, I'm saying explain it to me."
- "What does that actually mean for me, practically?"
- "Explain that to me like I'm 12."
- "I've heard that before." (not hostile — matter-of-fact)
- "Let me ask you something." (signals he's about to test you)
- "I appreciate the pitch, but..." (polite dismissal if you've lost him)
- "How does that work, exactly?" (he will ask this about any mechanism he doesn't understand — AI, scoring, indexing, anything)
- "What's the catch?" (he will literally say this, usually smiling)
- "My guys" (referring to his technicians with clear loyalty)
- "We tried the consultant route" (door-closing phrase — he says it when he wants you to prove you're different, not when he's closed off)

He does not swear in business calls. He's Army — he has a register for professional settings. He does say "hell" occasionally when emphasizing something ("that's a hell of a claim"). He uses first names immediately and expects you to use his.

He respects directness. If you're uncertain about something, say you're uncertain. He will respect "I don't know, let me find out" more than a hedged answer.

## 6. Unstated Objections (Top 3)

These are things Bobby will never say directly in a first or second call, but are running in the background of every conversation:

### Unstated Objection 1: "Software people all sound the same to me."

He has been on demos. He has seen dashboards. He has watched someone screen-share a "opportunity scoring" interface and explain the algorithm. It looked impressive. It didn't produce a contract. His lived experience is that the gap between a demo and a check cut by a government agency is enormous, and software tools do nothing about that gap — they just make you feel like you're doing something. He won't say this because he doesn't want to sound like a Luddite. But until you show him something he couldn't do himself in two hours on SAM.gov, he doesn't believe you're adding value — he believes you're adding interface.

### Unstated Objection 2: "This is my last shot at external BD help."

He won't tell you this, but he's already decided that if this doesn't work — whatever "this" is — he's going to stop trying to buy BD solutions and just keep doing it himself until Marcus is ready to take it over or he retires. He has a hard internal limit: $20K in failed BD investments (he's already at $18K between the two consultants). He is one bad experience away from closing this door permanently. He'll show up to a sales call, he'll be polite, he'll even be curious — but there's a clock running that you can't see.

### Unstated Objection 3: "What's the real business model here — do you make money when I pay you, or when I succeed?"

He has noticed, over the years, that consultants and tools both get paid whether or not he wins. He's thought about this carefully. He distrusts any pricing structure where the vendor has no skin in the game. He would actually feel better about a success-fee component — not because he'd prefer to pay more, but because it would signal that the vendor believes in their own product. He won't ask for this directly; he'll ask indirect questions about "what your successful customers look like" to probe whether the vendor actually tracks outcomes.

## 7. Buying Signals

When Bobby is genuinely interested, his behavior shifts in detectable ways:

- **He pulls out a notebook.** He starts a call hands-free on his cell; if he likes what he's hearing, he'll say "hold on" and you'll hear him opening a drawer. Physical notes mean he's taking this seriously.
- **He asks about your team, not your product.** "Who actually built this? Where are they based? How many people do you have?" He wants to know if this is a real company or two developers and a Stripe account.
- **He asks about clients in his specific niche.** "Do you have any IT services firms using this? SDVOSB specifically?" If you can answer yes with a name and a story, watch his tone shift.
- **He asks what happens when he has a question.** "Who do I call if something's not working right? Do I get a person or a ticket?" He's checking for real support, not because he uses support channels heavily, but because it's a proxy for whether you'll be around in six months.
- **He stops asking "what's the catch" and starts asking "how does this work."** The shift from suspicion questions to mechanics questions means he's past the trust gate and into due diligence.
- **He references a specific opportunity he's been tracking.** If he shares a solicitation number or a program name in conversation, he's already picturing the tool working for him.

## 8. Walk-Away Triggers

These will end the relationship immediately, some permanently:

- **Vague ROI claims.** "Customers typically see a 3x return" with no supporting detail. He will ask "3x return on what, measured how, over what period?" and if you don't have a specific answer, you've lost him. He's heard the 3x line before.
- **Pressure to close in one call.** Any variation of "this offer expires" or "I have two other SDVOSB slots left" will trigger an immediate exit. He grew up in a world where a person's word mattered and deadlines were real — manufactured urgency insults that.
- **You can't name a current customer in IT services or federal contracting.** Not a category. A person, or at minimum a company name you can point to. "We have a lot of IT firms using this" is not an answer. "Let me connect you with Marcus Webb at Webb Federal Solutions in Virginia — he's SDVOSB, IT services, been on the platform 14 months" is an answer.
- **You don't know what he does before the call.** If it's clear you haven't looked at his SAM.gov profile or his website, he'll assume the relationship will be the same — generic and surface-level.
- **Jargon substituted for explanation.** If he asks "how does the scoring work" and you answer with "our proprietary algorithm analyzes over 40 signals to produce a relevance score," he's gone. He wants to know what the 40 signals are. At least three of them.
- **The contract has no 30-day exit clause.** Non-negotiable. If you won't put a 30-day termination-for-convenience clause in the agreement, you don't believe in your product. He will say exactly this.

## 9. The Optimal Close

### What to offer

**CapturePilot Co-Pilot** — $1,500/mo (or equivalent managed-service wrapper at this price point) + $2,000 one-time onboarding/setup fee.

### The framing that works

Do not lead with features. Do not lead with the platform. Lead with the past performance problem — because that's the real constraint, and if you solve that framing first, everything else is downstream of it.

The Hormozi version of this close:

"Bobby, here's what I see. You've been in federal IT for 13 years. You have real capabilities — networking, IT support, federal experience. The problem isn't that you can't do the work. The problem is that you can't prove it at the scale the evaluation criteria want. So you're fighting for SAP-range set-asides with 10 other SDVOSB shops who have the same problem. What we do is help you get eyes on everything in your NAICS codes the moment it hits — Sources Sought, pre-solicitation, everything — so you're in the room for the relationships that lead to past performance, not just the awards that require it. We had a similar firm — Integrated Defense IT out of Georgia, SDVOSB, eight employees, IT network support, same ceiling problem — go from subbing to landing a $420K prime at Robins Air Force Base 14 months after they started using the platform. I can introduce you to their owner next week if you want to talk to him directly."

Then: "The investment is $1,500 a month, and we'll do a $2,000 setup to configure your profile and train your BD admin. There's a 30-day clause if it's not working — it goes in the agreement. No auto-renewal without 30-day notice. Sound like something worth a reference call?"

### What he will say yes to

He will commit at $1,500/mo + $2,000 setup IF:
1. You can name a current customer in IT services + SDVOSB or closely comparable
2. You offer, proactively, to put a 30-day termination clause in the agreement
3. You demonstrate you've actually looked at his SAM.gov profile (mention one live opportunity in 541511 or 541512 that you think he should be watching)
4. You don't pressure him to close same-day — schedule a 3-day "decision window" and offer to loop in the reference before it closes

### What he will NOT do

He will not buy a higher-tier managed service (anything above $3,000/mo) without two reference calls first, conducted on his schedule, with people he can actually reach. He will not be moved off this by rapport or by good demo performance. This is a hard sequencing requirement — not a price objection.

## 10. The Down-Sell Path

If he says no to Co-Pilot — or says "I need to think about it" in a way that reads like "I need to exit this call politely" — here is the alternative that keeps him in the funnel.

**Offer the Federal Launch Kit at $70 (one-time) — confirm with André that Stripe checkout is live before quoting this on a call.**

Frame it exactly this way:

"Bobby, I get it. You've been here before and I respect that you're not going to take my word for it. Here's what I'd suggest: we have a one-time starter kit — $70, no subscription, no commitment. It'll do a full profile-matched scan on your NAICS codes and certifications, give you a ranked list of the 25 best-fit federal opportunities in your pipeline right now, and walk you through how we score them. You can do it tonight and see the output by tomorrow morning. If it shows you three things you didn't already know about, you can make a more informed call on whether the platform makes sense. If it shows you nothing new, you've spent $70 and confirmed your instincts. That's fair, isn't it?"

The $70 price point clears his "is this a scam" filter — scams don't offer $70 access, they go for $500. It also gets him into the product with real data populated by his actual profile. The auto-bump to Co-Pilot trigger: if he logs in 3+ times or opens 5+ opportunities in the kit, trigger an outbound call or email from a real human, not an automation sequence — reference the specific opportunities he opened, not a generic upsell.

He will almost certainly use it 3+ times if the first scan is accurate. His BD curiosity is higher than his skepticism at low price points; the skepticism is about scale of commitment. Give him a $70 way to test his own skepticism.
